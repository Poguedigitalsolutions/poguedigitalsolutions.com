# Siri AI Ships Monday.

*Apple Has Run This Play Before. Ask Motorola.*

On Monday, iOS 27 arrives, and Siri finally becomes something close to the assistant Apple promised us two years ago. Part of that new intelligence comes from Google, which agreed to be completely invisible inside the product. Apple pays roughly a billion dollars a year for access to the technology behind Gemini, and Google gets no logo, no branding, no relationship with the customer, on paper that looks like the partnership of the decade. I read it as a lease with a very carefully written exit clause.

Look at how we got here. In February 2024, Apple killed Project Titan, its self-driving car, after ten years and more than ten billion dollars. There was no press release, just an internal announcement. About two thousand people were on that team. The software and machine-learning folks moved into Apple's AI group. Roughly six hundred others, the battery engineers and the test drivers, got layoff notices. Four months later, Apple Intelligence was announced at WWDC, and the story became a decade of car research redirected into AI, all that talent pointed at something better. I spent ten years at Cox Communications, so I know that language. You'd ask where Cindy went because you hadn't seen her all week, and management would tell you she got promoted to customer. Redirected is a word that only ever describes the people still in the building. Then the rollout came late and thin. The personal-context Siri that got the loudest applause never shipped. By March 2025, Siri had been taken from the executive who owned it and handed to the Vision Pro lead. That July, the man running Apple's foundation models team left for Meta on a package reported at over $200 million, and at least three more of his researchers followed him out within the month. In November 2025, Bloomberg reported Apple was shopping for outside help. In January, Google signed the deal. Two and a half years from "we've got this" to "we need to buy this."

What shipped in June was not one Google model but a family of five. Four are Apple's own, trained on Apple's infrastructure and then refined using Gemini's output. Only the top tier is built on the licensed Google foundation, and only that tier runs on Google's cloud. The contract term that should make Google's board sit up straight is the right to distill. Apple paid Google to teach models that Apple owns outright and gets to keep running long after the contract ends. Apple's own AI server chip goes into mass production this year, and Apple-operated data centers are penciled in for 2027.

If that pattern feels familiar, it should. In 2004 Apple partnered with Motorola to put iTunes on a phone, and Motorola's CEO was thrilled. The ROKR launched at an Apple event in September 2005, crippled at exactly one hundred songs, on a carrier deal Apple didn't control. At that same event, Apple introduced the iPod nano and buried its own partner's launch in the coverage. The press called the ROKR the "CROCKR." Sixteen months later, Apple announced the iPhone on Cingular, the same carrier it had just spent two years learning how to negotiate with. Cingular was rebranding as AT&T that same year, which is why we all remember it as the AT&T exclusive. Motorola got a punchline. Apple got an education, and that failure is exactly what convinced Steve Jobs to build the phone himself.

Google is not Motorola, and I'll say so plainly. Motorola never saw it coming. Google runs a frontier lab, knows this playbook cold, and signed anyway, partly to keep OpenAI from owning the assistant layer on a billion iPhones. Google also pays Apple around twenty billion a year for search placement, so Apple can't discard it the way it discarded a handset maker, and federal judges are watching this one. Google priced the risk with its eyes open.

Strip the differences away and the shape is the same one Imagination Technologies saw when it lost Apple's GPU business overnight, and the one Intel saw when it lost the Mac and then sold Apple its modem division. Apple rarely walks in the front door of a new category. It walks in through somebody else's, next to a partner who already knows the room, and it pays close attention on the way in. Motorola knew phones and carriers. Intel knew silicon. Google knows frontier AI. Apple borrows the experience, keeps the customer, keeps the brand, and starts building the replacement in the room next door.

That's patience, and patience is rare. But if you run a small business and a giant ever offers to partner with you, ask what they're learning from you and what you get to keep once they've learned it. When only one side walks away smarter, that's an apprenticeship, not a partnership, and you're the teacher.
