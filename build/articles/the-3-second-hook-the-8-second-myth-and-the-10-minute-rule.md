# The 3-Second Hook, the 8-Second Myth, and the 10-Minute Rule

## The Commercial That Got Me Thinking

I was watching TV when a Fandango commercial came on. It showed families struggling to get their kids to sit still. Then the family sits down together in front of a movie, and the kids don't move a muscle.

As an ad for Fandango and family movie night, it makes sense. A good story holds a child in place better than any rule a parent can make.

Then the commercial said something that stopped me in my tracks. If something doesn't hook you in 8 seconds, you're gone.

I've always taught 3 seconds. So I went looking for where the 8 came from, and what I found turned into a lesson about hooks, classrooms, and checking your sources.

## 3 Seconds or 8 Seconds

My 3-second number has a study behind it. Facebook asked the research firm Nielsen to look at 173 of its brand studies on Facebook video ads. People who saw an ad for 3 seconds or less still showed a 47% lift in ad recall, a 32% lift in brand awareness, and a 44% lift in purchase intent.

Facebook's own summary put it this way: up to 47% of a video campaign's value arrived in the first 3 seconds, and up to 74% arrived in the first 10. Facebook even counts a video "view" at 3 seconds.

The 8-second number has a much weaker story behind it. I'll get to that below, because it ties into everything else I found.

## The 10-Minute Rule From the 1970s Classroom

The hook gets attention. Keeping it is the second half, and teachers have worked on that since the 1970s.

- **1971:** Donald Bligh published *What's the Use of Lectures?*, a book still cited by teachers today. In later work, he measured students' pulses during class.
- **1976:** Two researchers, Johnstone and Percival, visited 90 college lectures. They reported attention dipping in the first five minutes and again 10 to 18 minutes in.
- **1978:** Hartley and Davies found students took fewer notes after 10 to 15 minutes of a lecture.

Decades later, molecular biologist John Medina turned this into the 10-minute rule in his 2008 bestseller *Brain Rules*. He teaches at the University of Washington School of Medicine and breaks his hour-long lectures into 10-minute segments, one core idea each.

Here's how I teach it. I coach people on affiliate marketing and social media so they can make money online, and the timing depends on what they're making.

- **A short video, around 2 minutes:** you have 3 seconds to hook them. That opening decides whether viewers keep scrolling or stay. Marketers call this "stopping the scroll."
- **A long lecture or training:** hook them early, then give about 10 minutes to one point before you change something: a story, a question, a demonstration, or a new voice. Then the clock starts over.

## Coach Castle's Earth Science Class

I loved science so much in high school that I took science classes as electives I didn't need. My Earth Science teacher was Coach Castle, a 6'4" football player who used his scholarship to study science instead of taking an easy major.

One day he was teaching the basics of planets and stars. I already knew most of it, and I wanted to give my classmates a chance. He asked a question and I looked around the room. Nobody raised a hand.

So I raised my hand and answered. "Good." He asked another, and I answered again. "Good." Question after question, my hand was the only one in the air.

Finally, he asked one more. As soon as my hand started to go up, he said, "Anybody but John! Answer this question!"

I smiled and lowered my hand, not in defeat, but in success. Somebody else finally spoke up, and the whole discussion opened up. More students joined in, and the room came alive.

Coach Castle didn't change the subject. He changed the voice. That one sentence reset the room better than any new slide could have, and it's the kind of change the 10-minute rule is asking for.

## Where the Numbers Fall Apart

When I dug into the sources, most of these numbers got shaky.

**The 8-second "goldfish" number:** This is the famous claim that human attention dropped from 12 seconds in 2000 to 8 seconds, one second shorter than a goldfish. It traces back to a 2015 report by Microsoft Canada. The figure didn't come from Microsoft's own research, though. The report credited a website called Statistic Brain, and no one has found the original data behind it. The British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC) investigated in 2017 and debunked it. There's also no evidence that goldfish have short attention spans. My guess is the Fandango writers pulled their 8 from this pool, since it's the most repeated version.

**The 10-minute rule:** In 2007, psychologists Karen Wilson and James Korn reviewed the classroom studies and found little support for a drop after 10 to 15 minutes. In 2016, Neil Bradbury, a professor of physiology at Rosalind Franklin University, found that the most often cited source barely discusses attention at all. The holes are easy to see:

- Johnstone and Percival watched only 13% of those lectures together, so most of the data came from one observer, and they never said how they measured attention.
- Students take notes fairly steadily, and the drop comes in the final ten minutes, not after the first ten.
- Bligh's pulse readings fell the same way in a discussion class as in a lecture.

**The "crap science":** You may also see a claim that adult attention fell from 12 minutes in 1998 to 5 minutes in 2008. It came from Lloyds TSB Insurance, whose PR team ran a survey of 1,000 people about careless household accidents. Nobody measured anything. People were simply asked how long their attention span was. The Telegraph and the Daily Mail both pulled the story within hours of posting it. It still turns up in respectable books, including reading researcher Maryanne Wolf's *Reader, Come Home*, which shows how far a weak number can travel.

**What holds up:** The 3-second finding comes from real ad campaigns where recall was measured. It shows that most of an ad's value lands early. It doesn't say people leave at second 4.

## Don't Believe Everything You Read. Look It Up.

The 8-second goldfish ran in major magazines and newspapers. The 10-minute rule sits in a bestseller. The 12-minute survey made it into a book about reading. A number being repeated doesn't make it true.

Before I repeat a statistic, I ask three questions:

1. Who measured it?
2. What did they measure?
3. Who paid for it, and what were they selling?

The Fandango commercial still got the most important part right. Kids who can't sit still will sit through a whole movie when the story is good. So hook people in 3 seconds, change something about every 10 minutes, and give them a story worth staying for. Then check your numbers before you share them.
